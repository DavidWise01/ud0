POLLARD’S p−1 METHOD if p-1 is smooth, one gcd falls out the factor

To factor n, exploit a weak prime factor p whose p-1 is smooth (only small prime factors). Compute a^M mod n for a highly composite M = lcm(1..B): if (p-1) ∣ M then a^M ≡ 1 (mod p) by Fermat, so p ∣ a^M - 1, and gcd(a^M - 1, n) hands you p. A targeted factoriser — and the reason RSA primes must have a large factor in p-1.

THE TECHNIQUE gcd(a^lcm(1..B) - 1, n)

Factor n = 253. Its factor 11 has p-1 = 10 = 2×5, smooth for B = 5. With M = lcm(1..5) = 60, gcd(2^60 - 1, 253) reveals 11. Watch the modular power and the gcd extract the factor: live demo







  
HISTORY & CREDIT John Pollard, 1974

  
“Pollard’s p−1 and Pollard’s rho are the same method.” — no. p−1 (1974) exploits a smooth p−1 via modular exponentiation; rho (1975) uses cycle detection (Floyd, dart 040) on a pseudorandom walk mod p. Same author, two different attacks with two different weaknesses. cited

  
1974 · John M. Pollard — “Theorems on factorization and primality testing”: the p−1 method.
1975 · Pollard — the rho method (cycle detection), a separate attack.
1987 · Hendrik Lenstra — the elliptic-curve method (ECM) generalises p−1: replace the group (Z/p)* of order p-1 with an elliptic curve whose order you can vary until it is smooth.

  
This is why safe primes exist: an RSA prime p is chosen so p-1 has a large prime factor, denying the p−1 method any smooth M to divide it — the attack shapes the key generation. Pollard 1974






  
RECOMMEND FOR I-13 the factor, computed

  
On the canonical compiler gcd(2^60 - 1, 253) extracts the factor 11 — bitwise modexp and Euclid’s gcd:

  
$ i13 run pollard.i13   # n = 253 = 11 x 23, B = 5, M = lcm(1..5) = 60
2^60 mod 253 = 78
gcd(78 - 1, 253) = gcd(77, 253) = 11   -- 11 falls out (p-1 = 10 divides 60)

  
Recommend: Pollard’s p−1 is LIT on I-13 and closes the corpus’s crypto loop — verified it factors 253 = 11×23 by computing 2^60 mod 253 = 78 and gcd(77, 253) = 11, using the same bitwise modexp and Euclid’s gcd the corpus already runs (RSA 050, extended-Euclid). It is the attack that motivates safe primes in RSA key generation — and a clean demonstration that a factoriser is just a well-chosen modular power plus a gcd. Its generalisation to elliptic curves (Lenstra ECM) is the modern engine; the p−1 shape is where it starts.
