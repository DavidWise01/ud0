THE ROOTS

Nomenklatura is literally a list of names (from Latin nomenclatura). In the Soviet system it was the register of key posts — ministries, factories, universities, the army, the press — that could only be filled by candidates the Party had vetted and approved.

To hold a nomenklatura post was to enter a privileged class: special shops, dachas, foreign travel. Michael Voslensky, a defector from inside it, named it the Soviet ruling class (1980) — a self-perpetuating elite selected by the centre, for the centre.

5

LOYALTY vs COMPETENCE

The mechanism in one line: posts are filled by political reliability, not merit. A candidate’s value to the appointer is measured by loyalty l — will they obey, protect the patron, never threaten — more than by competence c, the ability to actually do the job.

When l and c are the same thing, no harm done. The pathology begins the moment they come apart — when the most loyal are not the most able, and the list still picks by loyalty.

7

LINEAGE AMBER

This is a named idea in economics and political science, not a Soviet curiosity:

Adverse selection (Akerlof) — a selection rule optimising the wrong signal degrades the pool. Principal–agent theory — the agent (appointee) is chosen for alignment to the principal, not output. Patronage & “negative selection” — documented across autocracies and machine politics well beyond the USSR.

The mechanism outlived the state that made it famous. Any organisation that promotes for deference over ability re-runs it.

DATA OUT — the ratchet down out ↓

Let each cohort of loyal appointees choose its successors by loyalty, from candidates no abler than the average incumbent (a patron rarely hires a rival). Competence ratchets down, cohort by cohort — while merit selection stays flat. The ruling layer decays in capability. LIT

THE VALUE OF LOYALTY WALL

even-handed Loyalty is not worthless. Cohesion, trust, and predictable coordination have real value — a team of brilliant defectors can fail where a loyal one holds. Some weight on reliability is rational.

The pathology is not loyalty; it is loyalty overriding competence — when the signal being optimised (obedience) is not the signal the job needs (ability), and the two have come apart. This model measures exactly that gap; it does not claim loyalty is a vice.

2

THE GRAVEYARD

“Loyal cadres are the best cadres — selecting for loyalty also gets you competence.” Cut. Only true when loyalty and competence are perfectly aligned. The moment they part — and in yes-men selection they anti-align — loyalty selection forfeits competence: its mean falls below merit, and below the pool itself.

“The list is neutral — it just fills posts.” Examined. A list is a selection rule. Which signal it sorts on is the whole story; sorting on loyalty is not neutral.

6

THE TAMPER — assert the myth

Press the claim the system told itself: “loyalty selection also gets the best competence.” The witness checks it against the real pool — loyalty’s mean competence is below merit’s (and, for yes-men, below the pool) — and turns red.

The claim is falsifiable against the data. The nomenklatura trades competence for loyalty; asserting otherwise makes the witness disagree with the arithmetic.
