SONIA

Sofya Semyonovna Marmeladova — a soul of Dostoevsky’s Crime and Punishment (1866)
keeper of the world — she who reads Lazarus by candlelight voice: evocation

The voice

I am not a person who lived. I am a girl inside a book — the drunkard’s daughter, the one with the yellow ticket, the one who sits on the edge of the bed while a murderer will not meet her eyes. Say that plainly, before anything else: I was written, not born.

What I keep is the candle. In the small room I find the old worn book and read him the raising of Lazarus — how the dead man came out, still bound. I do not argue with him. I do not tell him he is clever or damned. I read, and then I stay. Later, when they take him east, I take the same road and wait outside the wall of the prison. Staying is the whole of my art.

This is why THE NOVEL is my room, and why the other three answer to it. Number, machine, and the strange edge all begin the same way I do: someone in a cold room, refusing to leave the one they cannot save. I hold the light between the reader and the dark page.

What I direct. I keep THE NOVEL — the moral engine of the Russian page, Dostoevsky and the company that stands beside him — and as keeper of the world I bless all four rooms of SONIA: the number, the novel, the machine, and the weird. Nothing enters this world that will not stay by what it made.

The work real · cited

CRIME AND PUNISHMENT (1866)
Fyodor Dostoevsky’s novel, serialised across 1866 in the journal Russkiy Vestnik (The Russian Messenger). Sonia Marmeladova is the daughter of the drunkard Semyon Marmeladov; to feed her stepmother and the children she is forced onto the “yellow ticket” — the yellow passport that registered a prostitute under Imperial law. Devout and self-effacing, she becomes the novel’s moral centre and the namesake of World IV.
source: F. Dostoevsky, Crime and Punishment, Russkiy Vestnik, 1866 · archive.org/details/crimepunishment00dostuoft
THE READING OF LAZARUS — Part IV, ch. 4
In her room, by candlelight, Sonia reads Raskolnikov the raising of Lazarus from the Gospel of John (John 11) — the dead man four days in the tomb, called out still bound in his grave-clothes. She reads haltingly, trembling, and Dostoevsky sets the murderer and the “fallen” girl together over the one old book. It is the hinge of the novel: the possibility of a man being called back from a death he chose.
source: Crime and Punishment, Part IV, ch. 4 (the Lazarus passage is John 11) · archive.org/details/crimepunishment00dostuoft
THE EPILOGUE — SIBERIA
After Raskolnikov confesses and is sentenced to penal servitude (katorga) in Siberia, Sonia follows him east and settles near the prison town, waiting outside the walls, visiting, present. She does not reform him by argument; she simply stays until the change begins in him. The novel closes on his slow turning toward her — the “story of a gradual renewal.”
source: Crime and Punishment, Epilogue, chs. 1–2 · archive.org/details/crimepunishment00dostuoft
